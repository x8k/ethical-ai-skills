# Patterns — Women and Economics

*Techniques, methods, and design patterns from Charlotte Perkins Gilman's "Women and Economics"*

---

## Economic Analysis Pattern

**When to use**: Analyzing social phenomena, gender roles, or inequalities

**How**:
1. Identify the economic conditions underlying the phenomenon
2. Trace how these conditions shape daily activities and behaviors
3. Examine the resulting social structures and adaptations
4. Distinguish natural developments from economic distortions

**Trade-offs**:
- + Reveals structural causes of social problems
- + Enables targeted, effective solutions
- - Requires overriding moral/emotional interpretations
- - May challenge deeply held beliefs

**Example**: Analyzing marital conflict → Identify economic dependence → Trace to limited autonomy → Examine power imbalances → Recognize as structural, not moral

---

## Sexuo-Economic Deconstruction

**When to use**: Addressing gender inequalities or sex-related social issues

**How**:
1. Separate the sex-relation from the economic relation
2. Identify where economic dependence combines with sexual relations
3. Analyze the effects of this combination on both sexes
4. Design solutions that address economic structures, not just behaviors

**Trade-offs**:
- + Reveals the unique human condition of combined relations
- + Explains persistent social problems
- - Requires discomforting analysis of intimate relations
- - Challenges traditional justifications for gender roles

**Example**: Prostitution → Recognize as economic dependence manifestation → Analyze as symptom of sexuo-economic relation → Address through economic independence, not moral condemnation

## Pathology of Social Evolution

**When to use**: Diagnosing social "diseases" (inequalities, conflicts, inefficiencies)

**How**:
1. Identify the social symptom (e.g., prostitution, marital discord)
2. Trace the symptom to its economic causes
3. Determine if the cause is natural or unnatural
4. Prescribe structural remedies rather than moral treatments

**Trade-offs**:
- + Treats social problems as solvable, not inevitable
- + Focuses on root causes
- - May be seen as reducing complex issues to economics
- - Requires systemic thinking over individual blame

**Example**: High divorce rates → Identify economic dependence → Trace to limited options → Recognize as unnatural economic condition → Prescribe economic independence

## Historical Entrenchment Recognition

**When to use**: Analyzing long-standing social conditions

**How**:
1. Identify the condition's historical origins
2. Distinguish between natural laws and historical contingencies
3. Recognize cultural normalization at work
4. Separate the condition's persistence from its rightness

**Trade-offs**:
- + Enables change by revealing conditions as human creations
- + Counters fatalism about social problems
- - May threaten vested interests in maintaining status quo
- - Requires historical knowledge

**Example**: Women's domestic role → Trace to historical economic developments → Recognize as entrenched, not natural → Enable rethinking of gender roles

---

## Economic Independence Framework

**When to use**: Designing solutions to gender inequalities

**How**:
1. Identify areas of economic dependence
2. Design systems that enable self-sufficiency
3. Ensure equal access to economic resources
4. Value and compensate previously unpaid labor

**Trade-offs**:
- + Creates lasting structural change
- + Benefits entire society, not just women
- - Requires significant economic and social restructuring
- - May face resistance from those benefiting from current system

**Example**: Maternity support → Recognize reproductive labor as economic → Design compensation systems → Enable women's economic independence

---

## Social Reconstruction Method

**When to use**: Redesigning social and economic systems

**How**:
1. Map current social positions and economic roles
2. Identify artificial divisions and inefficiencies
3. Design integrated roles based on aptitude, not sex
4. Implement through policy and cultural change

**Trade-offs**:
- + Increases overall social productivity
- + Creates more equitable society
- - Requires comprehensive vision and planning
- - Long-term process with resistance

**Example**: Workplace integration → Eliminate sex-based job restrictions → Design roles based on skills and interests → Implement equal opportunity policies

---

## Conceptual Precedence Strategy

**When to use**: Driving social change

**How**:
1. Identify harmful social conceptions
2. Develop alternative concepts and values
3. Disseminate new conceptions through education and media
4. Use conceptual change to enable structural transformation

**Trade-offs**:
- + Creates foundation for lasting change
- + Addresses cultural lag
- - Slow process requiring patience
- - May face conceptual conservatism

**Example**: Gender roles → Challenge "natural" assumptions → Promote equality concepts → Enable policy changes toward gender equity

---

*Derived from Charlotte Perkins Gilman's analytical methods in "Women and Economics" (1898)*
