# Glossary of Terms from Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals

**Alphabetical listing of key Kantian terms with definitions and locations.**

---

## A

- **A priori** — Knowledge or judgment independent of experience. The categorical imperative is an a priori principle of practical reason (Section II, 4:414-420).
- **Action (Handlung)** — A deed done with awareness of the moral law. Moral worth depends on the maxim of the action, not the action itself (Section I, 4:393-406).
- **Analytic Judgment** — A judgment where the predicate is contained in the subject (e.g., "All bachelors are unmarried"). Contrasts with synthetic judgment (Preface, 4:387-388).
- **Antinomy** — A contradiction between two seemingly valid principles. Practical reason avoids antinomies by having primacy over theoretical reason (Section III, 4:450-463).
- **A posteriori** — Knowledge or judgment derived from experience. Contrasts with a priori (Preface).
- **Autonomy** — The property of the will by which it is a law to itself. The foundation of all moral laws and of the dignity of rational nature (Section III, 4:432-445). **See also**: Autonomy Formula, Heteronomy.
- **Autonomy Formula** — The third formulation of the categorical imperative: "So act that your will can regard itself at the same time as making universal law through its maxims." (4:434). **See also**: Categorical Imperative.

---

## C

- **Categorical** — Unconditional, applying universally without qualification. The categorical imperative commands what ought to be done, period (Section II, 4:414-416). **See also**: Hypothetical, Categorical Imperative.
- **Categorical Imperative** — The supreme principle of morality: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law." (4:421). Has three main formulations (Section II). **See also**: Universal Law Formula, Humanity Formula, Autonomy Formula.
- **Contradiction in Conception** — A type of contradiction that arises when a maxim, if universalized, would make itself impossible. Found in the lying promise example (Section II, 4:422).
- **Contradiction in Will** — A type of contradiction that arises when a maxim, if universalized, would contradict an end that we necessarily will. Found in suicide, neglecting talents, and refusing to help examples (Section II, 4:422-430).

---

## D

- **Determination of the Will** — The process by which the will is moved to action. In autonomy, the will determines itself; in heteronomy, it is determined by external factors (Section III, 4:441-444).
- **Dignity (Wurde)** — Absolute inner worth. Humanity has dignity, not price, and must be treated as an end in itself (Section II, 4:429-430). **Contrast**: Price.
- **Duty (Pflicht)** — The necessity of acting from respect for the moral law. An action has moral worth only if done from duty, not from inclination (Section I, 4:397-400).

---

## E

- **End in Itself** — Something that has value in itself, not as a means to something else. Rational beings (humanity) are ends in themselves (Section II, 4:429). **See also**: Means to an End.
- **Empirical** — Based on or derived from experience. Empirical philosophy includes practical anthropology; empirical principles cannot ground morality (Preface, Section II).
- **Ethics** — The doctrine of morals, divided into empirical (practical anthropology) and pure (metaphysics of morals) parts (Preface, 4:387-392).

---

## F

- **Formal Philosophy** — Philosophy concerned with the form of understanding and reason itself. Logic is the only formal philosophy (Preface, 4:387). **Contrast**: Material Philosophy.
- **Freedom** — The property of the will by which it is not determined by sensible impulses. A postulate of pure practical reason (Section III, 4:446-447). **See also**: Postulates of Pure Practical Reason.
- **Formula of Universal Law** — First formulation of the categorical imperative: "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law." (4:421). **See also**: Categorical Imperative.

---

## G

- **Good Will (guter Wille)** — "It is impossible to think of anything at all in the world, or indeed even beyond it, that could be considered good without limitation except a good will." (4:393). The only thing good in itself (Section I). **See also**: Will.

---

## H

- **Happiness** — The satisfaction of all inclinations. In the highest good, happiness is united with virtue, but it is not the supreme good in itself (Section III, 4:439-445).
- **Highest Good** — "The world in which the greatest possible happiness is combined with the greatest possible conformity of rational beings to the moral law." The ultimate object of pure practical reason (Section III, 4:439-445). **See also**: Virtue, Postulates of Pure Practical Reason.
- **Heteronomy** — The property of the will by which it is determined by any law except the one it gives to itself. The opposite of autonomy (Section III, 4:440-444). **Types**: Sensibility, Rational Self-Interest, External Authority.
- **Humanity (Menschheit)** — The rational nature in persons; the capacity to set ends and act according to maxims. Has dignity and must be treated as an end in itself (Section II, 4:429). **See also**: Humanity Formula.
- **Humanity Formula** — Second formulation of the categorical imperative: "Act in such a way that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in the person of any other, never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end." (4:429). **See also**: Categorical Imperative.
- **Hypothetical Imperative** — A command of the form "If you will X, then you ought to do Y." Conditional on desires; not moral (Section II, 4:414-416). **Types**: Problematic, Assertoric. **Contrast**: Categorical Imperative.

---

## I

- **Inclination (Neigung)** — A desire that arises from feeling (sensibility) rather than from reason. Actions done from inclination have no moral worth, even if they conform to duty (Section I, 4:397-400). **Contrast**: Duty.
- **Immortality** — The endless existence of the soul. A postulate of pure practical reason, necessary for achieving perfect virtue (holiness) (Section III, 4:444-445). **See also**: Postulates of Pure Practical Reason.
- **Imperative** — A command of reason. Either hypothetical (conditional) or categorical (unconditional) (Section II, 4:414-420).

---

## K

- **Kingdom of Ends** — A systematic union of rational beings through common objective laws that they give to themselves. The ideal moral community (Section II, 4:433-439). **See also**: Autonomy Formula, Categorical Imperative.

---

## L

- **Law (Gesetz)** — A principle that applies universally. The moral law is the categorical imperative, a law that the will gives to itself (Section II, III).
- **Legislation (Gesetzgebung)** — The act of giving laws. In autonomy, each rational being is a legislator of the moral law (Section II, III).
- **Logic** — The science of the formal rules of thinking. The only formal philosophy; has no empirical part (Preface, 4:387).

---

## M

- **Material Philosophy** — Philosophy concerned with objects and the laws they follow. Divided into physics and ethics (Preface, 4:387). **Contrast**: Formal Philosophy.
- **Maxim (Maxime)** — The subjective principle of volition; the rule that an agent gives to themselves for action. The categorical imperative tests maxims, not individual actions (Section II, 4:420-421). **See also**: Categorical Imperative.
- **Means to an End** — Using something as a tool for achieving another purpose. Persons must never be treated merely as means to an end (Section II, 4:429). **Contrast**: End in Itself.
- **Metaphysics** — The rational (a priori) part of philosophy. Metaphysics of nature and metaphysics of morals are the two rational parts of material philosophy (Preface, 4:387-392).
- **Metaphysics of Morals** — The rational part of ethics; the subject of the Groundwork. Establishes the supreme principle of morality a priori (Preface, throughout).
- **Moral Law (moralisches Gesetz)** — The categorical imperative considered as a law. The law of autonomy (Section II, III).
- **Morality (Moralitat)** — The conformity of actions to the moral law. Distinct from legality (conformity to external laws) (Section I, II).

---

## N

- **Nature (Natur)** — The sensible world governed by causal laws. Morality must be based on freedom, not nature (Section III, 4:446-447).
- **Necessity** — The property of a law that it cannot be otherwise. The moral law has the necessity of a categorical imperative (Section II, 4:414-420).

---

## O

- **Ought (Sollen)** — The expression of moral obligation. The categorical imperative tells us what we ought to do (Section II).

---

## P

- **Physics** — The science of the laws of nature. Divided into empirical and rational (metaphysics of nature) parts (Preface, 4:387-388).
- **Postulates of Pure Practical Reason** — Necessary conditions for the possibility of the highest good: Immortality, Freedom, God (Section III, 4:444-450). **See also**: Highest Good.
- **Practical** — Concerned with action or what ought to be. Practical reason determines the will (Preface, Section II, III). **Contrast**: Theoretical.
- **Practical Anthropology** — The empirical part of ethics; the study of how humans actually act. Distinct from metaphysics of morals (Preface, 4:388-389).
- **Practical Reason (praktische Vernunft)** — Reason as the faculty for determining the will. Pure practical reason determines the will a priori (Section II, III). **See also**: Primacy of Practical Reason.
- **Price (Marktpreis, Tauschwert)** — Conditional worth; the value of something that can be replaced by something else. Contrasts with dignity (Section II, 4:429-430). **Contrast**: Dignity.
- **Primacy of Practical Reason** — The priority of practical reason over theoretical reason in determining the will. When they conflict, practical reason must have primacy (Section III, 4:450-463).
- **Principle** — A subjective rule (maxim) or objective rule (law). The categorical imperative is the supreme principle of morality (Section II, III).

---

## R

- **Rational Being (vernunftiges Wesen)** — A being with the capacity for reason. All rational beings have dignity and are ends in themselves (Section II, III).
- **Reason (Vernunft)** — The faculty of principles. Theoretical reason knows what is; practical reason determines what ought to be (Preface, Section II, III). **See also**: Pure Reason.
- **Respect (Achtung)** — The feeling that arises from the recognition of the moral law's authority. The motive of the good will (Section I, 4:400-401). **Contrast**: Inclination.

---

## S

- **Schema** — In theoretical reason, the procedure for connecting categories to phenomena. Analogous to the typic in practical reason (Section III, 4:436-437). **See also**: Typic of Pure Practical Judgment.
- **Sensibility (Sinnlichkeit)** — The capacity for sensations and desires. The source of inclinations and heteronomous determination (Section I, III). **Contrast**: Reason.
- **Supreme Good** — That which is good without limitation. The good will is the supreme good (Section I, 4:393). **See also**: Good Will, Highest Good.
- **Synthetic Judgment** — A judgment where the predicate is not contained in the subject but adds to our knowledge (e.g., "All bodies are heavy"). The categorical imperative is a synthetic practical judgment (Preface, 4:388).

---

## T

- **Theoretical Reason** — Reason as the faculty for knowing what is. Distinct from practical reason (Preface, Section III). **Contrast**: Practical Reason.
- **Typic of Pure Practical Judgment** — The procedure for subsuming particular actions under the universal moral law. Analogous to the schema in theoretical reason (Section III, 4:436-437).

---

## U

- **Understanding (Verstand)** — The faculty of rules; the capacity to think discursively. Distinct from reason (Preface, 4:387-388).
- **Universal Law** — A law that applies to all rational beings without exception. The categorical imperative requires that maxims be universalizable (Section II, 4:421). **See also**: Universal Law Formula.
- **Universal Law Formula** — **See**: Formula of Universal Law.

---

## V

- **Virtue (Tugend)** — The conformity of the will to the moral law. The supreme condition of the highest good (Section III, 4:439-445). **Contrast**: Happiness.
- **Volition (Wollen)** — The act of willing. The good will is good through its volition alone, not through what it accomplishes (Section I, 4:394).

---

## W

- **Will (Wille)** — The faculty of desire considered as the capacity to determine oneself to action in accordance with the representation of laws. The good will is a will that acts from duty (Section I, 4:393-406). **See also**: Good Will, Autonomy, Heteronomy.
- **Wisdom** — In the context of the postulates, the property of God as a wise author of nature who distributes happiness according to virtue (Section III, 4:447-450).

---

## Postulates of Pure Practical Reason
**Section III, 4:444-450** — Necessary conditions for the possibility of the highest good:
1. **Immortality** — The endless existence of the soul for perfect conformity to the moral law
2. **Freedom** — The will's independence from sensible causation
3. **God** — A wise and just author of nature who ensures happiness is proportionate to virtue

## Types of Heteronomy
**Section III, 4:441-444** — Three ways the will can be determined by external laws:
1. **Heteronomy of Sensibility** — Determined by desire (principle of happiness)
2. **Heteronomy of Rational Self-Interest** — Determined by prudent self-love (principle of one's own happiness)
3. **Heteronomy of External Authority** — Determined by another's commands (principle of obedience)

## The Three Formulations of the Categorical Imperative
**Section II, 4:421-439** — Three ways of expressing the same supreme principle of morality:
1. **Universal Law Formula** (4:421) — "Act only according to that maxim whereby you can, at the same time, will that it should become a universal law."
2. **Humanity Formula** (4:429) — "Act in such a way that you treat humanity... never merely as a means to an end, but always at the same time as an end."
3. **Autonomy Formula / Kingdom of Ends** (4:431, 4:439) — "So act that your will can regard itself... as making universal law" / "All maxims... ought to harmonize with a possible kingdom of ends."

## The Four Examples
**Section II, 4:421-430** — Four maxims tested against the categorical imperative:
1. **Lying Promise** — Impermissible (contradiction in conception)
2. **Suicide from Despair** — Impermissible (contradiction in will)
3. **Neglecting Talents** — Impermissible (contradiction in will)
4. **Refusing to Help Others** — Impermissible (contradiction in will)

## Division of Philosophy
**Preface, 4:387-388** — Kant's organizational scheme:
```
Philosophy
├── Formal (concerned with form of thinking)
│   └── Logic
└── Material (concerned with objects)
    ├── Physics (laws of nature)
    │   ├── Empirical (physics proper)
    │   └── Rational (metaphysics of nature)
    └── Ethics (laws of freedom)
        ├── Empirical (practical anthropology)
        └── Rational (metaphysics of morals)
```
