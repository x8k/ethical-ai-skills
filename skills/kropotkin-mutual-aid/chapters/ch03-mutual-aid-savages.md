# Chapter 3: Mutual Aid Among Savages

## Core Idea
Human societies at their earliest stages (what Kropotkin calls "savages") demonstrate extensive mutual aid patterns, particularly in the form of primitive communism where property is held in common and labor products are shared according to need.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Primitive Communism**: Social organization based on common ownership and equal distribution
- **Tribal Mutual Aid**: Co-operative structures in hunter-gatherer societies

## Key Concepts
- **Common Property**: Land, tools, and resources held collectively by the group
- **Shared Labor Products**: Hunting, gathering, and crafting results distributed according to need, not contribution
- **Rotating Leadership**: Authority based on merit and temporary need, not permanent hierarchy
- **Collective Decision-Making**: Consensus-based governance in tribal societies

## Mental Models
- **Use common ownership as default assumption**: Think of early human property relations as communal, not individualistic
- **Evaluate contribution by social benefit**: Prefer measuring individual worth by contribution to group welfare over accumulation of personal wealth

## Anti-patterns
- **Hobbesian State of Nature**: The view that early human life was a war of all against all; contradicted by extensive anthropological evidence of co-operation
- **Individual Property Assumption**: Projecting modern property concepts backward onto early societies ignores the prevalence of communal ownership

## Key Takeaways
1. Primitive communism is the natural state of early human societies
2. Common property and shared labor enable survival in harsh environments
3. Leadership in tribal societies is typically merit-based and temporary
4. Collective decision-making ensures all voices are heard in matters affecting the group
5. The transition from primitive communism to private property marks a significant social regression
