# Patterns - Mutual Aid: A Factor of Evolution

## Mutual Aid in Animal Societies
**When to use**: Analyzing cooperative behavior in non-human species
**How**: Document instances where animals of the same species support each other for survival, reproduction, or defense
**Trade-offs**: Requires field observation; laboratory studies may miss social context; but provides strongest evidence for mutual aid as natural law
**Examples**: 
- Herd animals defending young together
- Bird colonies sharing food and nest-building duties
- Insect societies with specialized co-operative roles

---

## Primitive Communism
**When to use**: Studying early human societies and property relations
**How**: Identify patterns of common ownership, shared labor, and collective decision-making in hunter-gatherer and early agricultural societies
**Trade-offs**: Historical evidence is fragmentary; anthropological observations may be influenced by modern biases; but reveals how humans naturally organize without hierarchy
**Examples**:
- Tribal land held in common
- Hunting parties sharing proceeds equally
- Rotating leadership based on merit, not inheritance

---

## Village Community Pattern
**When to use**: Analyzing pre-modern agricultural settlements
**How**: Examine the folkmote (assembly), communal land use, and mutual support networks that characterized early villages
**Trade-offs**: Evidence varies by region; many communities were disrupted by state formation; but provides model for decentralized organization
**Examples**:
- Germanic village communities
- Russian mir (peasant commune)
- Medieval European manorial systems with collective elements

---

## Guild System
**When to use**: Studying medieval economic organization and mutual support
**How**: Analyze how craft guilds regulated trades, provided mutual aid, and maintained quality standards
**Trade-offs**: Guilds could be exclusionary; some became hierarchical; but demonstrated sustainable co-operative economic models for centuries
**Examples**:
- Medieval European craft guilds
- Merchant guilds regulating trade
- Mutual support during illness or old age

---

## Artel (Russian Co-operative)
**When to use**: Understanding modern applications of mutual aid in labor organization
**How**: Study Russian artels where workers pool resources, share risks, and divide profits co-operatively
**Trade-offs**: Requires high trust among members; vulnerable to external competition; but demonstrates viability of co-operative labor in modern contexts
**Examples**:
- Fishing artels on Caspian Sea tributaries
- Railway porters' artels
- Building trade artels of 10-200 members

---

## Medieval City Mutual Support
**When to use**: Analyzing urban co-operative institutions before the rise of the modern state
**How**: Examine how medieval cities organized defense, trade, sanitation, and poor relief through mutual aid principles
**Trade-offs**: Cities were not utopias; inequality existed; but provided extensive networks of support that modern states often fail to match
**Examples**:
- Collective defense walls
- Mutual fire insurance
- Poor relief funded by guild contributions

---

## Modern Mutual Aid Networks
**When to use**: Applying historical patterns to contemporary social organization
**How**: Identify and support modern institutions based on mutual aid principles, from co-operatives to mutual aid societies
**Trade-offs**: Face competition from hierarchical, capitalist institutions; require conscious organization; but provide resilient alternatives
**Examples**:
- Lifeboat associations
- Friendly societies
- Credit unions
- Worker co-operatives
- Housing co-operatives

---

## Anarchist-Communist Organization
**When to use**: Designing stateless, classless social structures
**How**: Apply mutual aid principles to create voluntary associations for production, distribution, and governance
**Trade-offs**: Requires cultural shift away from competition; faces opposition from established powers; but offers path to truly free and equal society
**Examples**:
-Collective ownership of means of production
- Direct democracy through assemblies
- Federation of communes
- Free distribution according to need

---

## Anti-Pattern: Social Darwinism
**When to use**: Countering justifications for hierarchy and inequality
**How**: Expose the scientific errors in applying narrow competition models to human society
**Trade-offs**: Social Darwinist arguments are emotionally appealing to some; require education to counter; but are based on misinterpretation of Darwin's own views
**Examples**:
- "Survival of the fittest" interpreted as justification for cutthroat capitalism
- Opposition to social welfare based on "natural" competition
- Imperialism justified as evolutionary necessity

---

## Anti-Pattern: State Suppression of Mutual Aid
**When to use**: Analyzing how hierarchical institutions disrupt natural co-operative tendencies
**How**: Document how states break down village communities, guilds, and other mutual aid institutions to centralize power
**Trade-offs**: States provide some security and coordination; but at cost of freedom, equality, and natural sociability
**Examples**:
- Enclosure of common lands in England
- Destruction of German village independence by emerging states
- Suppression of guild autonomy by royal charters
