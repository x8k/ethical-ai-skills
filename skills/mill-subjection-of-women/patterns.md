# Patterns — The Subjection of Women

*Argumentation techniques and reform patterns from John Stuart Mill's essay*

---

## Principle of Perfect Equality Pattern

**When to use**: Evaluating any social arrangement or law regarding gender

**How**:
1. Identify the current social relation or law
2. Ask: does it create power, privilege, or disability based on sex?
3. If yes, it violates the principle of perfect equality
4. Propose replacement with arrangement admitting no sex-based power/privilege/disability

**Trade-offs**:
- + Clear, absolute standard that's easy to apply
- + Reveals injustices that might be hidden by gradations
- - May seem radical or utopian to some
- - Requires complete rejection of all sex-based distinctions

**Example**: Marriage laws → Do they give husbands power over wives? → Yes → Violates perfect equality → Reform to remove all sex-based power

---

## Hindrance to Improvement Analysis

**When to use**: Making the case for social reform

**How**:
1. Identify the injustice (women's subjection)
2. Show how it prevents human improvement:
   - Stifles development of half of humanity's capabilities
   - Maintains harmful social arrangements
   - Prevents optimal social organization
3. Argue that removing the hindrance would accelerate progress

**Trade-offs**:
- + Appeals to self-interest (everyone benefits from progress)
- + Shows injustice as practical problem, not just moral issue
- - Requires demonstrating causal connection
- - May be seen as overstating the impact

**Example**: Women's occupational exclusion → Prevents use of women's talents → Society loses potential contributions → Removing barriers would double available talent

## Custom vs. Principle Test

**When to use**: Evaluating traditional social arrangements

**How**:
1. Identify the custom or tradition
2. Identify the rational principle that should govern the area
3. Compare the custom to the principle
4. If they conflict, the principle should prevail

**Trade-offs**:
- + Elevates reason over tradition
- + Provides objective standard for evaluation
- - May be seen as dismissing cultural values
- - Requires identifying and defending the principle

**Example**: Women's exclusion from politics → Custom: only men participate → Principle: equal representation → Conflict → Principle should prevail

## Power Creates Abuse Pattern

**When to use**: Analyzing institutions with power imbalances

**How**:
1. Identify the power imbalance in an institution
2. Show how the superior party has legal or social power over the inferior
3. Demonstrate that this power is frequently abused
4. Argue that the only solution is to eliminate the power imbalance

**Trade-offs**:
- + Explains persistent problems in hierarchical institutions
- + Shows structural nature of abuse
- - Can be seen as anti-authority or radical
- - May overlook beneficial uses of power

**Example**: Marriage laws → Husbands have legal power over wives → This power is often abused (domestic tyranny) → Solution: equalize power in marriage

## True Nature Discovery Pattern

**When to use**: Responding to claims about inherent gender differences

**How**:
1. Identify the claim about "women's nature" or inherent differences
2. Show how current characteristics are shaped by social conditions
3. Argue that true nature can only be known under freedom
4. Conclude that we must create conditions of freedom to discover true capabilities

**Trade-offs**:
- + Undermines essentialist arguments
- + Opens up possibilities for human development
- - May be seen as denying any biological differences
- - Requires faith in human potential

**Example**: Claim: women are naturally less ambitious → Current: women socialized to be modest → Conditions: restricted opportunities → True nature: unknown under freedom → Conclusion: must test under equal conditions

## Utilitarian Benefit Calculation

**When to use**: Making practical case for women's freedom

**How**:
1. Identify the proposed change (women's freedom)
2. Calculate benefits:
   - Direct benefits to women
   - Benefits to men (improved relationships, etc.)
   - Benefits to society (increased talent pool, etc.)
3. Address potential costs
4. Show that benefits far outweigh costs

**Trade-offs**:
- + Appeals to practical, self-interested reasoning
- + Provides concrete reasons for support
- - May be seen as reducing moral issues to calculations
- - Requires quantifying sometimes intangible benefits

**Example**: Women's education → Benefits: women's development, better mothers, improved society → Costs: some initial disruption → Conclusion: benefits outweigh costs

---

## Institutional Reform Pattern

**When to use**: Designing reforms to social institutions

**How**:
1. Identify the institution (marriage, employment, etc.)
2. Analyze how it currently creates inequality
3. Propose reforms that:
   - Remove all sex-based power/privilege/disability
   - Create genuine equality of opportunity
   - Maintain beneficial functions of the institution
4. Implement gradually if necessary, but with clear goal of full equality

**Trade-offs**:
- + Provides clear path for change
- + Maintains social stability through gradual implementation
- - May be seen as too radical or too slow
- - Requires careful design to maintain institution's benefits

**Example**: Marriage reform → Current: husband has legal power → Goal: equal partnership → Steps: equal property rights, equal divorce rights, equal parental rights

## Harm Principle Application to Gender

**When to use**: Evaluating restrictions on women's freedom

**How**:
1. Identify the proposed restriction on women
2. Ask: who is harmed by women's freedom in this area?
3. If no one is harmed, the restriction is unjustified
4. If only the woman herself is "harmed," it's not a valid harm (self-regarding action)
5. Only restrictions preventing harm to others are justified

**Trade-offs**:
- + Clear, consistent standard for evaluating restrictions
- + Protects individual liberty
- - May be seen as too permissive
- - Requires distinguishing self-harm from other-harm

**Example**: Restriction: women can't work outside home → Harm to others: none → Harm to women: prevented from earning → Conclusion: restriction unjustified
