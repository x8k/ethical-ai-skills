## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Utopian Commonwealth (Book II)
**Use when**: Designing ideal societies, analyzing alternative economic systems, or evaluating social institutions.
**Core Idea**: Utopia is a communistic society where property is held in common, labor is required of all, and the needs of all citizens are provided for. This eliminates the social evils caused by private property.
**Key Features**:
  - No private property
  - All citizens work (6-hour workday)
  - Distribution according to need
  - Rational city planning
  - Religious tolerance
  - Rational governance

**Mental Model**: Think of a well-run household writ large — just as a family shares resources and each member contributes, so does the Utopian commonwealth.

### The Critique of Private Property (Book I)
**Use when**: Analyzing the causes of social inequality, crime, and political corruption.
**Core Idea**: Private property is the root cause of most social evils — theft, poverty, political corruption, and social conflict.
**The Argument**:
  - Where there is private property, people are never satisfied with what they have
  - The rich oppress the poor
  - The poor are driven to theft and crime to survive
  - Politics serves the interests of the wealthy
  - True justice requires the abolition of private property

**Mental Model**: Think of private property as a disease that infects all aspects of society. The cure is to establish a system where property is held in common.

### The Frame Narrative (Book I)
**Use when**: Understanding the literary structure of Utopia or analyzing More's method.
**Core Idea**: Utopia is presented through a nested frame narrative — More reports a conversation with Peter Giles, who introduces Raphael Hythloday, who describes Utopia.
**Structure**:
  - Outer frame: More as narrator
  - Middle frame: More's conversation with Peter Giles
  - Inner frame: Raphael Hythloday's account of Utopia

**Purpose**:
  - Creates distance between More and the radical ideas
  - Allows More to critique English society through Hythloday
  - Makes the social criticism more palatable

**Mental Model**: Think of Russian nesting dolls — each layer reveals another, with the smallest (Utopia) at the center.

### The Philosopher-King vs. the Practical Statesman (Book I)
**Use when**: Analyzing the role of philosophers in politics or the relationship between theory and practice.
**Core Idea**: Hythloday argues that philosophers should not serve princes because their counsel will be ignored or corrupted. The wise person should either keep silent or withdraw from court life.
**The Debate**:
  - **Hythloday**: Philosophers at court are either ignored or corrupted; they should not serve
  - **More**: Philosophers have a duty to serve and try to improve society
  - **Giles**: Agrees with More but acknowledges the difficulties

**Mental Model**: Think of a choice between:
  - The ivory tower (pure philosophy, detached from society)
  - The corrupt court (philosophy compromised by power)
  - The engaged reformer (philosophy working for gradual improvement)

### Economic Rationalism (Book II)
**Use when**: Designing efficient economic systems or evaluating the division of labor.
**Core Idea**: Utopia's economy is designed with rational efficiency — everyone works, work is evenly distributed, and surplus is stored for times of need.
**Key Principles**:
  - **Universal Labor**: Everyone works (except those exempted by age or scholarship)
  - **Short Workday**: 6 hours of work per day
  - **Division of Labor**: Citizens rotate through different jobs
  - **No Idleness**: Even the Syphogrants (magistrates) work
  - **Surplus Production**: Extra goods are stored and shared with other nations

**Mental Model**: Think of a well-tuned machine where each part contributes efficiently to the whole.

### Religious Tolerance (Book II)
**Use when**: Analyzing models of religious coexistence or the separation of church and state.
**Core Idea**: Utopia practices almost complete religious tolerance, with most citizens believing in a single God and rational religion, but allowing diversity of belief.
**Key Features**:
  - Most Utopians believe in one God and an afterlife
  - They respect other people's religious beliefs
  - A small number believe in multiple gods or no god
  - priests are elected and can be removed for misconduct
  - Religion supports moral behavior

**Mental Model**: Think of a garden with many different flowers — each is beautiful in its own way, and none should be trampled.

### Rational City Design (Book II)
**Use when**: Planning cities, architecture, or urban development.
**Core Idea**: Utopian cities are designed with rational principles for efficiency, beauty, and social cohesion.
**Key Features**:
  - **Uniform Design**: All cities have the same layout
  - **Central Market**: Goods are distributed from a central location
  - **Family Housing**: 30 families live together in a block
  - **Public Spaces**: Gardens, hospitals, and meeting halls
  - **Clean and Healthy**: Streets are wide, houses have gardens

**Mental Model**: Think of a city as a living organism — each part has its function, and the whole is designed for health and efficiency.

### The Abolition of Money (Book II)
**Use when**: Analyzing alternative economic systems or the role of money in society.
**Core Idea**: Utopia has no money, no poverty, and no wealth. Goods are distributed according to need, not according to ability to pay.
**Consequences**:
  - No theft (nothing to steal)
  - No poverty (everyone's needs are met)
  - No social classes based on wealth
  - No greed or avarice
  - Trade with other nations uses gold and silver (which Utopians despise)

**Mental Model**: Think of money as a social construct that creates more problems than it solves. Utopia shows what society would look like without it.

### Social Equality (Book II)
**Use when**: Analyzing class systems or designing egalitarian societies.
**Core Idea**: Utopia is a society of equals where distinctions of wealth and social class do not exist.
**Key Features**:
  - **Equal Distribution**: Everyone receives the same goods
  - **No Luxury**: No one lives in excessive luxury
  - **Rotation of Labor**: Everyone performs the same types of work
  - **Meritocratic Leadership**: Leaders are chosen for wisdom, not birth
  - **No Servants**: Even magistrates serve meals to each other

**Mental Model**: Think of a level playing field where everyone starts with the same opportunities and resources.

### The Role of the State (Book II)
**Use when**: Analyzing government functions or the relationship between the state and citizens.
**Core Idea**: The Utopian state provides for the welfare of all citizens while maintaining individual freedom and dignity.
**Key Features**:
  - **Welfare**: The state ensures everyone's basic needs are met
  - **Education**: Universal education in useful knowledge
  - **Healthcare**: Hospitals for the sick
  - **Work**: The state organizes labor but doesn't force it
  - **Freedom**: Citizens are free to pursue their interests within limits

**Mental Model**: Think of the state as a good parent — providing for needs, educating, and protecting, while allowing independence.
