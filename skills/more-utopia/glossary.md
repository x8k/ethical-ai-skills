# Glossary of Terms from Utopia

**Alphabetical listing of key Utopian terms with definitions and locations.**

---

## A

- **Ademus** — The title of the Prince (ruler) of Utopia. Originally called Barzanes. Elected for life from a list of four candidates nominated by the people. Has limited power and cannot act without the council (Book II). **See also**: Barzanes, Prince, Syphogrants, Tranibors.
- **Agriculture** — Organized system of food production in Utopia. Families from cities rotate to work on farms. Highly efficient and produces abundant food for all citizens (Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Common Storehouses.
- **Amaurot** — The capital city of Utopia, located near the center of the island. The political and administrative center where the annual council of senators meets (Book II). **See also**: Utopia (the island), Channel.
- **Atheism** — The belief in no god. In Utopia, a small number of citizens hold this belief. They are tolerated and allowed to practice their beliefs privately, though most Utopians believe in a single God (Book II). **See also**: Religion, Religious Tolerance.

---

## B

- **Barzanes** — The original title of the Prince of Utopia, later changed to Ademus (Book II). **See also**: Ademus, Prince.

---

## C

- **Channel** — The 15-mile waterway dug by King Utopus to separate Utopia from the continent. Serves as a defensive barrier and symbolizes Utopia's separation from the corrupt world (Book II). **See also**: Utopus, Amaurot, Geography of Utopia.
- **Cities of Utopia** — 54 identical cities scattered across the island. All have the same layout, institutions, and laws. Amaurot is the capital (Book II). **See also**: Amaurot, Rational City Design.
- **Common Storehouses** — Central warehouses where all goods are stored and from which they are distributed according to need. The economic heart of Utopia's communist system (Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Universal Labor, Distribution.
- **Communism** — The economic system of Utopia, where property is held in common and goods are distributed according to need. Contrasts with the private property systems of contemporary Europe (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Private Property (critique of), Common Storehouses.
- **Critique of Private Property** — Hythloday's argument in Book I that private property is the root cause of social evils including theft, poverty, political corruption, and social conflict. **See also**: Private Property, Sheep and Men Parable, Enclosure Movement.

---

## D

- **Division of Labor** — The system by which citizens rotate through different jobs in Utopia. Ensures variety, prevents specialization from becoming oppressive, and allows people to work according to their abilities and interests (Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Workday, Agriculture.
- **Distribution** — The system by which goods are allocated from common storehouses according to each citizen's needs. No money or trade is involved (Book II). **See also**: Common Storehouses, Abolition of Money, Surplus.

---

## E

- **Economic Rationalism** — The principle that Utopia's economy is designed with rational efficiency. Features include universal labor, short workday, division of labor, and surplus storage (Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Division of Labor, Surplus, Common Storehouses.
- **Education** — Universal and lifelong in Utopia. All children receive instruction in useful knowledge (agriculture, crafts), moral and civic education, and intellectual pursuits for those with ability. Those with scholarly talent are exempted from manual labor (Book II). **See also**: Leisure, Scholars.
- **Enclosure Movement** — The historical process in England of fencing off common land for private use, which impoverished many peasants. Criticized by Hythloday through the Sheep and Men Parable as an example of how private property causes social harm (Book I). **See also**: Critique of Private Property, Sheep and Men Parable, Private Property.
- **End in Itself** — In Utopian ethics, treating persons with inherent dignity. While not explicitly stated in these terms, the principle underlies Utopia's respect for all citizens and its rejection of exploitation (Book II). **See also**: Slavery, Religious Tolerance.

---

## F

- **Family** — In Utopia, extended families live together in large households of 10-16 adults. Marriage is regulated but based on mutual consent. Children are raised by their biological parents. Both men and women work (Book II). **See also**: Marriage, Household, Children.
- **Frame Narrative** — The literary structure of Utopia, consisting of multiple nested layers: (1) More as narrator, (2) More's conversation with Peter Giles, (3) Raphael Hythloday's account of Utopia. Creates distance between More and the radical ideas presented (Book I). **See also**: Thomas More (as character), Peter Giles, Raphael Hythloday.

---

## G

- **Geography of Utopia** — The physical characteristics of the island: crescent-shaped, 200 miles across at its widest point, separated from the continent by the Channel, surrounded by rocks and shallows, with a large central bay serving as a natural harbor. Rich soil and abundant resources (Book II). **See also**: Utopus, Channel, Amaurot.
- **Gold and Silver** — Precious metals despised by Utopians as useless and corrupting. Used only for foreign trade and for making chamber pots and chains for slaves (Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Slavery, Trade.

---

## H

- **Healthcare** — Free and available to all citizens in Utopia. Each city has several well-equipped hospitals staffed by skilled physicians. Focuses on prevention through public health measures and treatment of illness (Book II). **See also**: Hospitals, Public Health, Diet.
- **Hospitals** — Medical facilities in each Utopian city, providing free treatment to all citizens. Part of Utopia's comprehensive healthcare system (Book II). **See also**: Healthcare, Physicians.
- **Household** — The basic residential unit in Utopia, consisting of extended families living together. Originally 30-40 adults, later reduced to 10-16. A matron and patron oversee each household (Book II). **See also**: Family, Population Control.
- **Hythloday, Raphael** — The traveler and philosopher who describes Utopia. His name means "speaker of nonsense" or "knowing in trifles" (Greek), which is ironic given the profundity of his knowledge. Serves as More's mouthpiece for social criticism (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Frame Narrative, Thomas More (as character), Peter Giles.

---

## I

- **Idleness** — Considered a waste of human potential in Utopia. Unlike in Europe where many are unemployed, everyone in Utopia works (except children, the elderly, and scholars). Even magistrates (Syphogrants) work (Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Workday.

---

## L

- **Labor** — In Utopia, work is universal, organized, and efficient. Everyone works 6 hours per day, rotating through different jobs. Labor is the foundation of Utopia's prosperity (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Workday, Division of Labor.
- **Laws** — In Utopia, there are few laws because most disputes are settled by custom and reason. Laws exist to promote the common good, not to punish. Legal procedures are public, and punishments aim to reform (Book II). **See also**: Government, Tranibors, Syphogrants.
- **Leisure** — Citizens have significant free time after the 6-hour workday. Used for reading, intellectual pursuits, physical exercise, socializing, and attending public lectures (Book II). **See also**: Education, Entertainment.
- **Luxury** — Absent from Utopia. No one lives in excessive luxury, as it encourages greed, envy, and waste. Goods are distributed according to need, not desire (Book II). **See also**: Social Equality, Abolition of Money.

---

## M

- **Marriage** — In Utopia, men must be at least 22 and women at least 18 before marrying. Couples see each other naked before marriage to avoid disappointment. Both parties must consent. Divorce is permitted for adultery, intolerable perverseness, or desertion. Divorced persons can remarry, except adulterers who cannot marry their partners (Book II). **See also**: Family, Divorce, Adultery.
- **Matron** — A female household manager in Utopia. Works with the patron to oversee a household of extended families (Book II). **See also**: Household, Patron.
- **Money (critique of)** — Utopia has no money, which eliminates greed, poverty, theft, and social classes based on wealth. Money is seen as a social construct that creates artificial scarcity and social division (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Private Property (critique of), Common Storehouses.
- **More, Thomas (as character)** — The narrator of Utopia, representing the practical, engaged statesman. His presence allows More to distance himself from Hythloday's radical ideas. Defends the position that philosophers should serve princes and try to improve society (Book I). **See also**: Frame Narrative, Peter Giles, Raphael Hythloday.

---

## O

- **Official, Utopian** — Government positions in Utopia, all elected by the people. Include the Prince (Ademus), Syphogrants, and Tranibors. Most have limited terms and can be removed for misconduct (Book II). **See also**: Prince, Syphogrants, Tranibors, Election.

---

## P

- **Patron** — A male household manager in Utopia. Works with the matron to oversee a household (Book II). **See also**: Household, Matron.
- **Peter Giles** — More's friend in Antwerp, a humanist scholar. Connects More to Raphael Hythloday and facilitates their conversation. Represents the curious, open-minded humanist (Book I). **See also**: Frame Narrative, Thomas More (as character), Raphael Hythloday.
- **Philosophy** — In Utopia, philosophical inquiry is valued and those with scholarly ability are exempted from manual labor to pursue study. The role of philosophers in politics is debated in Book I (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Philosopher and the Court, Scholars.
- **Philosopher and the Court** — The debate in Book I among More, Giles, and Hythloday about whether philosophers should serve princes. Hythloday argues against it (counsel will be ignored or corrupted), while More defends it (duty to society, gradual reform possible). Giles acknowledges both positions (Book I). **See also**: Raphael Hythloday, Thomas More (as character).
- **Physics** — The branch of material philosophy concerned with the laws of nature. Mentioned in the context of dividing philosophy (Book I).
- **Population Control** — Mechanism for maintaining balanced population across Utopia's 54 cities. Families are moved between cities by lot every 10 years to maintain uniformity (Book II). **See also**: Cities of Utopia, Household.
- **Poverty** — Unknown in Utopia due to the abolition of private property and the system of common storehouses. Contrasts with contemporary Europe where poverty drives people to crime (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Common Storehouses, Critique of Private Property.
- **Prince** — The elected ruler of Utopia, also called Ademus (formerly Barzanes). Chosen for life from four candidates nominated by the people. Has limited power and cannot act without the council of Syphogrants (Book II). **See also**: Ademus, Barzanes, Syphogrants, Council.
- **Private Property (critique of)** — Central to Hythloday's social criticism in Book I. Argued to be the root cause of theft, poverty, political corruption, and social conflict. The alternative is a society without private property (Utopia) (Book I). **See also**: Critique of Private Property, Communism, Enclosure Movement.
- **Property** — In Utopia, all property is held in common, eliminating the social evils associated with private ownership. Goods are distributed according to need from common storehouses (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Common Storehouses, Abolition of Money, Communism.
- **Public Health** — Measures taken in Utopia to prevent illness, including clean living conditions, personal hygiene, regular exercise, and simple nutritious diet (Book II). **See also**: Healthcare, Hospitals, Physicians.
- **Public Spaces** — In Utopian cities: markets, hospitals, temples, and meeting halls. Designed for community use and social cohesion (Book II). **See also**: Rational City Design, Cities of Utopia.
- **Punishment** — In Utopia, punishments are designed to reform rather than harm. Serious crimes may result in slavery, but slaves can earn their freedom through good behavior (Book II). **See also**: Slavery, Laws, Reform.

## R

- **Rational City Design** — The principle that Utopian cities are designed with rational efficiency for health, beauty, and social cohesion. Features include uniform grid patterns, wide streets, standardized buildings, and abundant public spaces (Book II). **See also**: Cities of Utopia, Amaurot, Public Spaces.
- **Reason** — The foundation of Utopian society. Reason guides governance, economic organization, education, and even religious belief for most Utopians (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Natural Religion, Education.
- **Religion** — In Utopia, most citizens believe in a single, benevolent God and an afterlife. A small number believe in multiple gods or no god. Religious tolerance is practiced, with no persecution for beliefs. Religion supports moral behavior (Book II). **See also**: Religious Tolerance, Natural Religion, Temples.
- **Religious Tolerance** — The principle in Utopia that different religious beliefs are tolerated and respected. No one is compelled to believe anything, and no one is persecuted for their beliefs. All religions agree on basic moral principles (Book II). **See also**: Religion, Natural Religion, Atheism.
- **Return to Society** — The philosopher's obligation to use their wisdom for the common good, debated in Book I. More's position, contrasted with Hythloday's initial inclination to withdraw (Book I). **See also**: Philosopher and the Court, Raphael Hythloday, Thomas More (as character).

---

## S

- **Scholars** — In Utopia, those with intellectual ability are identified and exempted from manual labor to pursue scholarly study full-time. Education continues throughout life for all citizens (Book II). **See also**: Education, Universal Labor.
- **Sheep and Men Parable** — Hythloday's story in Book I about how sheep, which were once gentle and required little, now "devour" men and fields. A metaphor for the enclosure movement, where landlords' wealth (sheep) impoverishes peasants (Book I). **See also**: Enclosure Movement, Critique of Private Property.
- **Slavery** — Exists in Utopia for three categories: (1) Utopian criminals, (2) foreign criminals purchased by Utopia, and (3) poor people from other nations who choose slavery over poverty. Slaves perform unpleasant work, wear gold and silver chains, but can earn freedom. They are treated with basic dignity (Book II). **See also**: Slavery in Utopia, Gold and Silver, Punishment.
- **Slavery in Utopia** — One of the most controversial aspects of More's vision. While slavery exists, it is framed as a form of temporary punishment and reform rather than permanent exploitation. Slaves can earn their freedom (Book II). **See also**: Slavery, Gold and Silver.
- **Social Equality** — A defining feature of Utopia, where distinctions of wealth and social class do not exist. All citizens receive the same goods, work the same types of jobs, and have equal opportunities (Book II). **See also**: Abolition of Money, Universal Labor, Common Storehouses.
- **Spirit (to thumos)** — While not a central concept in Utopia as it is in Plato's Republic, the idea of a spirited or honor-loving part of human nature is present in the context of military training and defense (Book II). **See also**: War, Military Training.
- **State, Role of the** — In Utopia, the state provides for the welfare of all citizens while maintaining individual freedom and dignity. Functions include ensuring basic needs, education, healthcare, and work organization (Book II). **See also**: Government, Prince, Syphogrants, Tranibors.
- **Storehouses, Common** — See **Common Storehouses**.
- **Surplus** — Extra goods produced in Utopia beyond what is immediately needed. Stored in common storehouses for emergencies or shared with other nations through trade (Book II). **See also**: Common Storehouses, Trade, Universal Labor.
- **Syphogrants** — The 200 magistrates who govern Utopia alongside the Prince. Meet with the Prince to discuss state affairs. Originally three per city, later increased. Elected annually (Book II). **See also**: Prince, Tranibors, Government.

---

## T

- **Temples** — Beautiful public buildings for worship in Utopia. Used for public prayer in forms acceptable to all believers (Book II). **See also**: Religion, Religious Tolerance, Public Spaces.
- **Theft** — In Book I, Hythloday argues that punishing thieves is unjust when society (through private property and poverty) creates the conditions that drive people to theft. The solution is to eliminate those conditions rather than punish the symptom (Book I). **See also**: Critique of Private Property, Private Property, Sheep and Men Parable.
- **Thomas More (author)** — The author of Utopia and, as a character, the narrator. Through the frame narrative, he distances himself from the radical ideas while still presenting them for consideration (Book I). **See also**: Frame Narrative, Thomas More (as character).
- **Tranibors** — The 200 officials in Utopia who hear cases and settle disputes. Judicial branch of government. Elected annually by the people (Book II). **See also**: Prince, Syphogrants, Government, Laws.
- **Trade** — Utopia engages in limited trade with other nations, primarily to obtain resources they cannot produce themselves. Uses gold and silver (which they despise) as trade goods (Book II). **See also**: Gold and Silver, Surplus, Economy.

---

## U

- **Universal Labor** — The principle in Utopia that everyone works, except children, the elderly, and those exempted for scholarship. Eliminates idleness and ensures that all necessary work is performed efficiently (Book II). **See also**: Workday, Division of Labor, Idleness.
- **Utopia (the island)** — The crescent-shaped island described in Book II. Approximately 200 miles across at its widest point, separated from the continent by the Channel, with 54 identical cities. Founded by King Utopus (Book II). **See also**: Geography of Utopia, Utopus, Amaurot, Channel.
- **Utopia (the concept)** — From Greek ou-topos meaning "no place" or "nowhere." Suggests that More's ideal society may be unattainable, but worth striving for (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Utopia (the island).
- **Utopian Commonwealth** — The ideal society described in Book II, characterized by common property, universal labor, rational governance, religious tolerance, and social equality (Book II). **See also**: Communism, Social Equality, Economic Rationalism.
- **Utopus** — The legendary king who conquered the island of Utopia and separated it from the continent by digging the Channel. The founder of Utopian civilization and its rational organization (Book II). **See also**: Geography of Utopia, Channel, Amaurot.

---

## V

- **Virtue** — Moral excellence. In Utopia, the social and political organization is designed to cultivate virtue in citizens. Most Utopians practice basic moral principles regardless of their specific religious beliefs (Book II). **See also**: Religion, Education, Natural Religion.

---

## W

- **War** — Utopians prefer peace but are prepared for war when necessary. They only fight defensive wars or to liberate oppressed peoples. All citizens receive military training. Mercenaries are hired with gold and silver for foreign conflicts (Book II). **See also**: Military Training, Peace, Mercenaries.
- **Welfare** — The state in Utopia ensures that everyone's basic needs are met through the system of common property and distribution according to need (Book II). **See also**: State (Role of the), Common Storehouses, Universal Labor.
- **Work** — In Utopia, work is organized, efficient, and universal. The 6-hour workday is sufficient to produce all necessary goods because there is no idleness or inefficiency (Book I, Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Workday, Division of Labor.
- **Workday** — In Utopia, the standard workday is 6 hours, divided between morning and afternoon. This is sufficient because everyone works and work is well-organized (Book II). **See also**: Universal Labor, Division of Labor, Economic Rationalism.
