# Book X: The Rejection of Poetry

**Stephanus Pages**: 592c-621d | **Characters**: Socrates, Glaucon

## Core Idea
Plato concludes the Republic with a critique of imitative poetry and a myth about the afterlife. Poetry, especially tragedy and comedy, is dangerous because it appeals to the inferior parts of the soul and reinforces false beliefs. The just person should be rewarded not only in this life but also in the afterlife, as illustrated by the Myth of Er.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Critique of Imitative Poetry (595a-608b)
**One of Plato's most controversial claims — the banishment of poets from the ideal city.**

- **The Charge**: Imitative poetry corrupts the souls of its audience
- **Target**: Primarily tragedy and comedy (though all imitative poetry is suspect)
- **Reason**: Poetry appeals to and strengthens the inferior parts of the soul (spirit and appetite) rather than reason

#### The Three Beds Argument (596a-598e)
- **The Question**: What is the relationship between a bed (physical object), a painting of a bed (image), and the Form of Bed (reality)?
- **The Answer**:
  - God makes one Form of Bed (the true reality)
  - The carpenter makes one particular bed (participating in the Form)
  - The painter makes an image of a bed (an image of an image)
- **Hierarchy**:
  - Form of Bed (true reality) → Carpenter's bed (participation) → Painter's bed (image of image)
- **Problem**: The poet, like the painter, creates images that are three times removed from the truth

#### The Measure of Distance from Truth
- **Poetry's Status**: Poetry is at the bottom of the Divided Line (imagination, eikasia)
- **Comparison**:
  - The user of an object knows its truth
  - The maker of an object knows its truth
  - The imitator (poet/painter) knows only appearance, not truth
- **Conclusion**: Poetry does not deal with truth but with appearances

#### The Psychological Effect of Poetry (603c-608b)
- **The Problem**: Poetry does not just depict vice — it encourages identification with vice
- **Mechanism**: When we watch a tragic hero suffering, we:
  1. Feel pity for the hero
  2. Enjoy the pleasure of pity
  3. Strengthen our own capacity for pity (which is a form of weakness)
- **Similarly**: Comedy strengthens our capacity for laughter at vice
- **Effect**: Poetry "waters" the inferior parts of the soul (spirit and appetite), making them grow strong, while reason withers

- **The Banishment**:
  - Only hymns to the gods and praises of good people are allowed in the ideal city
  - All other poetry is banished
  - This includes Homer and the tragic poets

### The Myth of Er (614a-621d)
**Plato's final myth — a vision of the afterlife and the soul's journey.**

- **The Story**: Er, an Armeni an soldier, dies in battle but does not decay. Ten days later, he revives on the funeral pyre and tells of his journey to the afterlife.
- **The Afterlife Geography**:
  - A meadow with two openings in the sky and earth
  - Judges sitting between them
  - The good go up through one opening, the bad go down through the other
  - In the center: the Spindle of Necessity

#### The Spindle of Necessity (616b-617d)
- **Description**: A great spindle turned by the three Fates (daughters of Necessity):
  - **Lachesis**: Singing of the past
  - **Clotho**: Singing of the present
  - **Atropos**: Singing of the future
- **The Whorls**: Eight concentric circles on the spindle, each representing a heavenly body:
  1. Fixed stars
  2. Saturn
  3. Jupiter
  4. Mars
  5. Mercury
  6. Venus
  7. Sun
  8. Moon
- **The Sirens**: Each whorl has a siren that sings a single note, creating a harmony
- **The Turning**: The spindle turns on the lap of Necessity, maintaining the cosmic order

#### The Choice of Lives (617d-620d)
- **The Process**:
  1. Souls come before Lachesis
  2. They choose their next life and the daemon (guardian spirit) that will guide them
  3. Clotho draws the lot of life and ratifies the choice
  4. Atropos makes the choice irrevocable
- **The Order**: Souls choose in order of their previous life's virtue
- **The Problem**: Most souls choose poorly because they choose by appearance, not by reality
- **The Exception**: Only souls that have lived philosophically choose wisely

#### The Journey Back (620d-621b)
- **After Choice**: Souls drink from the River of Forgetfulness (Lethe) and forget everything
- **Rebirth**: They are reborn according to their choice
- **Er's Mission**: Er is sent back to tell this story to humanity

## Key Concepts

### The Defense of Justice (608c-614a)
- **The Challenge**: Glaucon asks for a defense of justice that does not rely on reputation or consequences
- **The Response**: Even if the just person is thought to be unjust and the unjust person thought to be just, the just person is still happier
- **The Analogy**: The just person is like a healthy person who is thought to be sick; the unjust person is like a sick person thought to be healthy. The healthy person is still better off.
- **Conclusion**: The just soul is in a healthy state, the unjust soul in a sick state

### The True Value of Poetry
- **What Poetry Does Well**:
  - Can praise the gods and good people
  - Can inspire virtue in the right context
- **What Poetry Does Poorly**:
  - Depicts the gods and heroes engaging in vice
  - Encourages identification with vice
  - Appeals to the irrational parts of the soul
  - Reinforces false beliefs about reality

### The Soul's Immortality (608d-611a)
- **The Argument**: The soul cannot be destroyed by its own vices (injustice, cowardice, etc.)
- **Reason**: If vice could destroy the soul, then justice would be the destruction of the soul (by destroying the unjust part), which is absurd
- **Conclusion**: The soul is immortal and must be cared for

### The Reward of Justice
- **In This Life**: The just person is happier, as proven in Book IX
- **In the Afterlife**: The just person is rewarded with a better next life
- **The Choice**: Only the philosopher, who has knowledge of the truth, can choose a truly good life

## Mental Models

- **Use the Three Beds Test**: When evaluating art or media, ask: how many times removed from truth is this representation?
- **Use the Soul-Watering Analogy**: When consuming media, ask: which part of my soul is this watering?
- **Use the Choice of Lives**: When making major life decisions, ask: am I choosing by appearance or by reality?
- **Use the Forgetfulness Principle**: Remember that most people forget the truth — this is why education and reminder are so important
- **Use the Spindle of Necessity**: To understand cosmic order, remember that all things are governed by necessity and the harmony of the spheres

## Anti-patterns

- **Mimetic Confusion**: Mistaking images for reality (confusing poetry with truth)
- **Soul Neglect**: Focusing on the body's health while neglecting the soul's health
- **Appearance Choosing**: Choosing a life based on appearances rather than reality (the mistake most souls make in the Myth of Er)
- **Poetic Corruption**: Using poetry to reinforce vice rather than virtue
- **False Pity**: Feeling pity for those who deserve their suffering (e.g., tragic heroes who are punished for their own vices)

## Key Takeaways

1. Imitative poetry is banished from the ideal city because it appeals to and strengthens the inferior parts of the soul
2. Poetry is three times removed from truth (Form → particular → image of particular)
3. The poet, like the painter, deals only with appearances, not with reality
4. Poetry encourages identification with vice, strengthening the capacity for vice in the audience
5. Only hymns to the gods and praises of good people are allowed
6. The soul is immortal and cannot be destroyed by vice
7. The just person is happier in this life (Book IX) and rewarded in the afterlife
8. The Myth of Er illustrates that most people choose their lives poorly because they are deceived by appearances
9. Only the philosopher, who has knowledge of the Forms, can choose a truly good life
10. The Republic ends with a vision of cosmic justice — the soul gets what it chooses, and the just soul chooses well
