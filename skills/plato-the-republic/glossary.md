# Glossary of Terms from The Republic

**Alphabetical listing of key terms with definitions and locations.**

--- 

## A

- **Adeimantus** — Son of Ariston, brother of Glaucon and Plato. Interlocutor in the Republic. Represents the skeptical but fair-minded young person (Ch I, II, V).
- **Allegory of the Cave** — Plato's most famous analogy (Book VII, 514a-520a): prisoners in a cave mistake shadows for reality; the philosopher escapes, sees the true world, and returns to enlighten others. **See also**: Cave, Sun Analogy, Divided Line.
- **Appetite (to epithumetikon)** — The lowest part of the soul, desiring food, drink, sex, and other bodily pleasures (Book IV, 439d-440a). Corresponds to the producer class in the city. **See also**: Tripartite Soul.
- **Aristocracy** — Rule by the best; the ideal city with philosopher-kings (Book VIII, 544c-545c). **See also**: Philosopher-King, Degenerate Regimes.

--- 

## B

- **Beautiful (to kalon)** — In Plato's philosophy, not just pleasing to the senses but participating in the Form of Beauty. The philosopher loves what is truly beautiful, not just what appears beautiful (Book V, 475d-480a).

--- 

## C

- **Cephalus** — Wealthy old man, father of Polemarchus. Represents conventional piety and unexamined moral beliefs (Book I, 328c-331d).
- **City-Soul Analogy** — The fundamental framework of the Republic: the city and the individual soul have the same structure and the same virtues (Book II, 368c-369a; Book IV, 434d-445a).
- **Courage (andreia)** — Virtue of the auxiliaries; preservation of correct beliefs about what is to be feared and not feared (Book IV, 429a-430c).
- **Craft (techne)** — Skilled knowledge with a proper function. Socrates argues that ruling, like medicine or piloting, is a craft that serves its object (Book I, 341c-342c). **See also**: Craft Analogy.
- **Craft Analogy** — The analogy that just as a doctor aims at the health of the patient (not the doctor's profit), a true ruler aims at the good of the city (Book I, 341c-342c).

--- 

## D

- **Daemon (daimon)** — Guardian spirit; in the Myth of Er, each soul chooses a daemon to guide its next life (Book X, 617d-620d).
- **Democracy** — Rule by the poor majority; the fourth regime in the sequence of decline (Book VIII, 555b-558c). Characterized by freedom and equality but lack of knowledge in rulers. **See also**: Degenerate Regimes.
- **Dialectic** — The philosopher's method: questioning and reasoning to ascend from hypotheses to first principles. The highest form of reasoning (Book VI, 531c-535a; Book VII, 532d-535a).
- **Divided Line** — Analogy illustrating four levels of reality and cognition (Book VI, 509d-511e): Imagination (images), Belief (physical objects), Thought (mathematical objects), Understanding (Forms). **See also**: Sun Analogy, Cave.

--- 

## E

- **Education (paideia)** — The formation of character through stories, music, gymnastics, and philosophy. Essential for producing good rulers (Book II-III, VII). **See also**: Music, Gymnastics.
- **Eikasia** — Imagination; the lowest cognitive state, dealing with images and shadows (Divided Line). **See also**: Pistis, Dianoia, Noesis.
- **Episteme** — Knowledge; cognition of the Forms (the intelligible world). **See also**: Doxa.
- **Er, Myth of** — The final myth of the Republic (Book X, 614a-621d): Er dies, sees the afterlife, and returns to tell of the soul's choice of lives. **See also**: Spindle of Necessity, Choice of Lives.

--- 

## F

- **Form (eidos/idea)** — The true reality, unchanging, perfect essence of which particular objects are copies. The objects of knowledge (Book V-VII). **See also**: Theory of Forms, Particular.
- **Form of the Good** — The highest Form, source of all reality and intelligibility (Book VI, 506d-509c). **See also**: Sun Analogy.

--- 

## G

- **Glaucon** — Son of Ariston, brother of Plato. Main interlocutor in the Republic. Represents the intelligent but skeptical young person (throughout).
- **Guardians** — The ruling class of the ideal city, divided into rulers (philosophers) and auxiliaries (soldiers). Selected for their natural ability and educated rigorously (Book II-VII).
- **Gymnastics** — Physical education; one of the two parts of education, balancing music. Develops the body to serve the soul (Book III, 403c-412b). **See also**: Education.

--- 

## H

- **Harmony** — The proper relationship among parts of a whole, whether city or soul. Justice is harmony (Book IV, 433a-434c).

--- 

## I

- **Imitation (mimesis)** — Representation of reality through art or poetry. Plato criticizes imitative poetry because it deals with appearances, not truth, and strengthens the inferior parts of the soul (Book III, 392d-398b; Book X, 595a-608b). **See also**: Three Beds Argument.

--- 

## J

- **Justice (dikaiosyne)** — The central concept of the Republic. Defined as each part doing its own work, both in the city and in the soul (Book IV, 433a-434c; 441c-442a). **See also**: Justice as Harmony.
- **Justice as Harmony** — Justice in city and soul is the harmonious functioning of parts where each performs its proper function (Book IV).

--- 

## K

--- 

## L

- **Lethe, River of** — The River of Forgetfulness in the Myth of Er. Souls drink from it before rebirth and forget their previous lives (Book X, 620d-621b).

--- 

## M

- **Mathematics** — Essential part of the philosopher's education (Book VII, 522c-531c). Includes arithmetic, geometry, stereometry, astronomy, and harmony. Prepares the soul for dialectic by turning it from the visible to the intelligible world.
- **Moderation (sophrosyne)** — Virtue of all classes; agreement about who should rule and be ruled (Book IV, 430d-432a). In the soul, harmony between reason, spirit, and appetite.
- **Music (mousike)** — Education of the soul through stories, poetry, and music. One of the two parts of education, balancing gymnastics (Book III, 376e-412b). **See also**: Education, Censorship of Poetry.
- **Myth of the Metals** — The "noble lie" that citizens are born with different metals (gold, silver, bronze, iron) in their souls, determining their proper role in the city (Book III, 414d-415d). **See also**: Noble Lie.

--- 

## N

- **Necessity, Spindle of** — Cosmic mechanism in the Myth of Er. A great spindle turned by the three Fates, representing the order of the heavens and the souls' choices (Book X, 616b-617d). **See also**: Myth of Er, Fates.
- **Noesis** — Understanding; the highest cognitive state, dealing directly with the Forms (Divided Line). **See also**: Episteme, Doxa, Eikasia, Dianoia.
- **Noble Lie (gennaion pseudos)** — A useful falsehood for social cohesion. The Myth of the Metals is the noble lie of the Republic (Book III, 414d-415d).

--- 

## O

- **Oligarchy** — Rule by the rich few; the third regime in the sequence of decline (Book VIII, 550c-555b). Characterized by exploitation of the poor by the rich. **See also**: Degenerate Regimes.

--- 

## P

- **Particular** — Individual physical object that participates in a Form. Particulars are changing, imperfect copies of Forms (Book V-VII). **See also**: Form, Theory of Forms.
- **Phaedo** — Interlocutor in Book I (328a). Not to be confused with the dialogue Phaedo.
- **Philosopher** — Lover of wisdom. In the Republic, the philosopher is the one who loves the sight of truth and has knowledge of the Forms (Book V-VII). **See also**: Philosopher-King, Lovers of Sights and Sounds.
- **Philosopher-King** — The ruler of the ideal city, who has both philosophical knowledge and political power (Book V, 473d-e). **See also**: Philosopher, Ship of State.
- **Pistis** — Belief; the cognitive state dealing with physical objects (Divided Line). **See also**: Episteme, Eikasia, Dianoia, Noesis.
- **Polemarchus** — Son of Cephalus. Interlocutor in Book I. Defines justice as giving to each what is appropriate (Book I, 331d-336b).
- **Poetry, Critique of** — Plato's argument that imitative poetry corrupts the soul by appealing to the inferior parts and reinforcing false beliefs (Book III, X). **See also**: Three Beds Argument.

--- 

## R

- **Reason (to logistikon)** — The highest part of the soul, loving truth, ruling the whole (Book IV, 439d-441c). Corresponds to the ruler class in the city. **See also**: Tripartite Soul.
- **Ring of Gyges** — A mythical ring that makes its wearer invisible. Glaucon uses it to argue that anyone would be unjust if they could get away with it (Book II, 359a-360d). **See also**: Glaucon's Challenge.

--- 

## S

- **Ship of State** — Analogy describing the city as a ship with an ineffective captain (the people), true pilots (philosophers) who are ignored, and false pilots (sophists) who flatter the captain (Book VI, 488a-489d). **See also**: Philosopher-King.
- **Socrates** — The main speaker in the Republic. Athenian philosopher, son of Sophroniscus. Historical figure and Plato's teacher.
- **Soul (psyche)** — The immortal principle of life and thought. Has three parts: reason, spirit, appetite (Book IV). **See also**: Tripartite Soul, Immortality of the Soul.
- **Spirit (to thumoeides)** — The middle part of the soul, loving honor, getting angry, supporting reason (or appetite) (Book IV, 439d-441c). Corresponds to the auxiliary class in the city. **See also**: Tripartite Soul.
- **Sun Analogy** — The Form of the Good is to the intelligible world as the sun is to the visible world (Book VI, 506d-509c). **See also**: Divided Line, Cave.

--- 

## T

- **Theory of Forms** — Plato's metaphysics: the physical world is a shadow of a higher, perfect, eternal reality. Forms (or Ideas) are the true reality; particular objects are imperfect copies (Book V-VII). **See also**: Form, Particular.
- **Thrasymachus** — Sophist from Chalcedon. Defines justice as the advantage of the stronger (Book I, 338c). Represents the cynical view that might makes right. **See also**: Thrasymachus' Challenge.
- **Thrasymachus' Challenge** — The claim that justice is the advantage of the stronger (Book I, 338c). Sets up the central problem of the Republic: proving that justice is good for its own sake. **See also**: Glaucon's Challenge.
- **Timocracy** — Rule by honor; the second regime in the sequence of decline (Book VIII, 545c-550c). Characterized by a military elite ruling for honor. **See also**: Degenerate Regimes.
- **Tripartite Soul** — The division of the soul into three parts: reason, spirit, appetite (Book IV, 434d-441c). **See also**: Reason, Spirit, Appetite.
- **Tyranny** — Rule by one cruel master; the worst regime (Book VIII, 562a-569c; Book IX, 571a-592b). **See also**: Degenerate Regimes, Tyrannical Soul.
- **Tyrannical Soul** — The soul ruled by a single base appetite, enslaving reason and spirit. The least happy of all souls (Book IX, 580a-588a). **See also**: Tyranny.

--- 

## U

- **Understanding (noesis)** — The highest cognitive state (same as noesis) (Book VI, 511d-e). **See also**: Divided Line.

--- 

## V

- **Virtue (arete)** — Excellence or goodness. In the Republic, the four cardinal virtues are wisdom, courage, moderation, and justice (Book IV). Each class of the city and each part of the soul has its proper virtue.

--- 

## W

- **Wisdom (sophia)** — Virtue of the rulers; knowledge of what is good for the whole city (Book IV, 428b-429a). In the soul, wisdom is the virtue of reason.

--- 

## Three Beds Argument
**Book X, 596a-598e** — Argument that the poet's product is three times removed from truth: God makes the Form of Bed, the carpenter makes a particular bed, the painter makes an image of a bed (an image of an image). **See also**: Critique of Poetry, Divided Line.

## Degenerate Regimes
**Book VIII-IX** — The four unjust regimes that arise from the decline of the ideal city: Timocracy, Oligarchy, Democracy, Tyranny. Each corresponds to a type of soul. **See also**: Sequence of Constitutions.

## Fates, The Three
**Book X, 616b-617d** — Daughters of Necessity who turn the Spindle of Necessity: Lachesis (past), Clotho (present), Atropos (future). **See also**: Myth of Er.

## Choice of Lives
**Book X, 617d-620d** — In the afterlife, souls choose their next life and daemon. Most choose poorly because they are deceived by appearances. **See also**: Myth of Er.

## Lovers of Sights and Sounds
**Book V, 475d-480a** — Those who love beautiful things but not Beauty itself, who love pleasant sounds but not Harmony itself. Contrasted with philosophers who love truth itself. **See also**: Philosopher.

## Glaucon's Challenge
**Book II, 358a-362c** — Three-part challenge to Socrates: (1) justice is a compromise, (2) anyone would be unjust if they could get away with it (Ring of Gyges), (3) justice is only valued instrumentally. **See also**: Thrasymachus' Challenge, Adeimantus' Reinforcement.

## Adeimantus' Reinforcement
**Book II, 362d-367b** — Reinforces Glaucon's challenge by describing two common but corrupting views of justice. **See also**: Glaucon's Challenge.
