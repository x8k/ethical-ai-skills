# What Is Property? - Chapter 2: Property Considered as a Natural Right

## Core Idea
Proudhon deconstructs the claim that property can be justified as a natural right, demonstrating that the concept of a natural right to property is logically incoherent and historically groundless.

## Frameworks Introduced
- **Natural Right Critique**: Systematic dismantling of the claim that property is a natural, inalienable right
- **Occupation Theory**: Analysis and refutation of the claim that occupation creates legitimate property rights

## Key Concepts
- **Natural Right Definition**: Rights that exist independently of human convention, based on universal reason or divine will
- **Occupation**: The act of taking possession of previously unowned land or resources
- **Civil Law**: The body of law created by society to regulate property relations
- **Definitions of Property**: Multiple attempts to define property and their inherent contradictions

## Mental Models
- **Use historical analysis to debunk natural claims**: Think of natural right claims as historically contingent rather than eternally valid
- **Occupation as arbitrary act**: Prefer understanding occupation as a contingent act that cannot create universal moral rights

## Anti-patterns
- **Naturalizing social conventions**: Treating historically developed property institutions as if they were natural and inevitable
- **Confusing legal with moral**: Equating what is legally recognized with what is morally justifiable

## Key Takeaways
1. No consistent definition of property as a natural right can be formulated
2. Occupation cannot create a legitimate title because it lacks universal applicability
3. Civil law does not validate property; it merely sanctions existing theft
4. The concept of natural rights applied to property is logically incoherent
5. Property as currently practiced contradicts any reasonable definition of natural rights
