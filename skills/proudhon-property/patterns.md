# Patterns - Property Is Theft!

## Mutualism Pattern
**When to use**: Designing economic systems based on reciprocity and justice
**How**: Create institutions that ensure equivalent exchange, eliminate exploitation, and base economic relations on mutual recognition
**Trade-offs**: Requires cultural shift from competition to co-operation; faces opposition from established capitalist interests; but provides stable, just economic foundation
**Examples**: Mutual banks, credit unions, worker co-operatives, consumer co-operatives

---

## Dialectical Analysis Pattern
**When to use**: Analyzing social and economic institutions to expose their internal contradictions
**How**: Identify the opposing principles within an institution, analyze their interaction, and propose a synthetic resolution
**Trade-offs**: Can be abstract and philosophical; requires careful application to concrete situations; but reveals hidden tensions and possibilities
**Examples**: Property (individual right vs. social theft), Competition (liberty vs. destruction), Monopoly (stability vs. oppression)

---

## Federalism Pattern
**When to use**: Organizing political power without hierarchy
**How**: Build from local communes upward through voluntary federation, with recallable delegates for specific purposes
**Trade-offs**: Requires high level of organization and trust; vulnerable to centralization if not carefully maintained; but provides democratic, decentralized governance
**Examples**: Commune federations, worker federations, international federations

---

## Mutual Credit Pattern
**When to use**: Providing capital and financial services without exploitation
**How**: Create banks that extend credit based on mutual guarantee rather than collateral, without charging interest
**Trade-offs**: Requires mutual trust and shared risk; vulnerable to default if members are not careful; but democratizes access to capital
**Examples**: People's Bank, mutual credit associations, co-operative banks

---

## Property Refutation Pattern
**When to use**: Deconstructing justifications for property as an institution
**How**: Systematically address each claimed foundation (natural right, occupation, labor, law, consent) and demonstrate its logical and moral inconsistencies
**Trade-offs**: Can be legally and philosophically complex; requires understanding multiple justificatory frameworks; but provides comprehensive demolition of property's claimed legitimacy
**Examples**: Refuting Lockean labor theory, natural rights claims, legal positivism

---

## Justice as Synthesis Pattern
**When to use**: Resolving social conflicts through equitable solutions
**How**: Identify legitimate claims on all sides of a conflict and create institutions that satisfy all legitimate interests
**Trade-offs**: Requires identifying what is truly legitimate vs. self-interested; can be difficult to implement in practice; but provides stable, long-term resolutions
**Examples**: Property reform (possession vs. appropriation), value theory (use-value vs. exchange-value), governance (liberty vs. order)

---

## Economic Evolution Pattern
**When to use**: Understanding historical development of economic institutions
**How**: Analyze how each economic stage (primitive communism, division of labor, machinery, competition, monopoly, police/taxation, credit) emerges from the contradictions of the previous stage
**Trade-offs**: Historical analysis can be speculative; requires understanding specific historical contexts; but reveals patterns of development and possibilities for transformation
**Examples**: Division of labor from communism, machinery from division of labor, competition from machinery, etc.

---

## Self-Organization Pattern
**When to use**: Enabling groups to manage their own affairs without external control
**How**: Develop local capacity for decision-making, implement direct democracy, use recallable delegation for broader co-ordination
**Trade-offs**: Requires education and practice; vulnerable to co-optation or inefficiency; but empowers communities and builds social capacity
**Examples**: Worker self-management, community self-governance, co-operative enterprises

---

## Anti-Pattern: Property as Natural Right
**When to use**: Identifying flawed justifications for property
**How**: Expose the historical contingency, logical inconsistencies, and social harms of natural rights claims to property
**Trade-offs**: Natural rights arguments are emotionally appealing; require education to counter; but are based on false premises
**Examples**: Lockean labor theory, divine right of kings applied to property, various philosophical justifications

---

## Anti-Pattern: Competition as Natural Law
**When to use**: Identifying flawed justifications for unregulated markets
**How**: Expose how competition is not an inevitable natural phenomenon but a social institution with specific effects that require regulation or replacement
**Trade-offs**: Competition has real benefits; requires careful analysis to distinguish between beneficial and harmful aspects; but unregulated competition creates instability and exploitation
**Examples**: Social Darwinism, laissez-faire capitalism, free market fundamentalism

---

## Anti-Pattern: State as Neutral Arbitrator
**When to use**: Identifying flawed understandings of state power
**How**: Expose how the state serves specific class interests (particularly capitalist and propertied classes) rather than acting as an impartial regulator
**Trade-offs**: State does provide some security and co-ordination; requires analysis of specific state actions; but state power is inherently connected to class rule
**Examples**: Police protection of property, taxation serving capitalist interests, state regulation favoring business

---

## Anti-Pattern: Hierarchical Authority
**When to use**: Identifying oppressive power structures
**How**: Expose how hierarchy concentrates power, removes it from those affected, and creates opportunities for abuse
**Trade-offs**: Hierarchy can provide efficiency and co-ordination; requires demonstrating alternatives; but hierarchical authority is inherently oppressive
**Examples**: Political hierarchy, economic hierarchy (capital over labor), social hierarchy (class, race, gender)

---

## Anti-Pattern: Exploitation as Natural
**When to use**: Identifying ideological justifications for economic oppression
**How**: Expose how exploitation is not a natural or inevitable feature of human relations but a product of specific social institutions (particularly property)
**Trade-offs**: Exploitation has been widespread historically; requires understanding its institutional basis; but is not natural or justified
**Examples**: Wage labor as fair exchange, profit as reward for risk, rent as payment for service
