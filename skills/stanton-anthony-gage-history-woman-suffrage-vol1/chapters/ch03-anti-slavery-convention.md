# Chapter 3: The World's Anti-Slavery Convention, London, June 12, 1840

## Core Idea
The World's Anti-Slavery Convention in London (1840) was a pivotal moment where women delegates were excluded from participation, catalyzing the formation of the independent women's rights movement. This exclusion revealed that even among reformers, gender prejudice remained strong.

## Frameworks Introduced

### The Intersectional Exclusion Framework
- **Core**: Even progressive movements can replicate patterns of exclusion and oppression
- **Mechanism**: Reform movements focused on one form of oppression may fail to recognize or challenge other forms
- **Application**: The anti-slavery movement's exclusion of women revealed the need for an independent women's rights movement

## Key Concepts
- **Lucretia Mott**: Quaker minister and abolitionist, delegate to the convention, excluded from participation
- **Elizabeth Cady Stanton**: Young abolitionist, delegate to the convention, excluded from participation
- **Double Battle**: Women abolitionists fought against both slavery and sexism simultaneously
- **Individualism vs. Authority**: The convention revealed conflicts between individualist principles and adherence to traditional authority
- **Bigoted Abolitionists**: Some abolitionists supported freedom for slaves but not equality for women
- **James G. Birney**: Abolitionist who supported freedom on plantations but not in his own home
- **Thomas Clarkson**: Venerable abolitionist received standing by the convention
- **William Lloyd Garrison**: Refused to sit in the convention in protest of women's exclusion
- **Debate on Female Delegates**: Lengthy debate over whether to seat women delegates
- **Rejection of Women**: The "Females" were ultimately rejected from participation

## Mental Models
- Use **intersectional analysis** when examining reform movements: look for patterns of inclusion and exclusion across different forms of oppression
- Think of **alliances as conditional**: alliance with one reform movement may require independent organizing on other issues

## Key Takeaways
1. The exclusion of women from the World's Anti-Slavery Convention was a turning point that revealed the need for independent women's rights organizing
2. Even progressive men could hold prejudiced views about women's proper role
3. The experience of exclusion at London connected Stanton and Mott, leading directly to Seneca Falls
4. The incident demonstrated that women could not rely on male-led reform movements to address their concerns
5. The "double battle" against both racial and gender oppression characterized many early women's rights activists
