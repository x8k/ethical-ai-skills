# Glossary — History of Woman Suffrage, Vol. I

**Abolitionists** — Activists working to end slavery; many were also women's rights advocates. The World's Anti-Slavery Convention (1840) was a key moment where women delegates were excluded (Ch 3)

**Anti-Slavery Convention (1840)** — World's Anti-Slavery Convention in London where women delegates including Lucretia Mott and Elizabeth Cady Stanton were refused seats, catalyzing the women's rights movement (Ch 3)

**Ballot** — The vote; central demand of the suffrage movement. Women sought the ballot as a means of self-protection and self-defense (Introduction, Ch 1)

**Brahmins** — In the caste analogy, men occupy the position of Brahmins (the highest caste), while women are Pariahs (the lowest) (Ch 1)

**Caste of Sex** — The systemic division of society where men and women have different rights, codes, and opportunities, analogous to the Indian caste system (Ch 1)

**Civil Rights** — Legal rights concerning property, contracts, marriage, and personal status. Women's civil rights improved in the 19th century but remained unequal (Ch 1, Ch 5, Ch 12)

**Consent of the Governed** — Principle that governments derive their just powers from the consent of the governed; central to the suffrage argument (Title page, Introduction)

**Declaration of Independence (1776)** — Document asserting that all men are created equal; women argued this principle applied to them as well (Introduction)

**Declaration of Sentiments** — Document adopted at the Seneca Falls Convention (1848) modeling the Declaration of Independence but asserting women's rights (Ch 4)

**Divine Right** — Claim that authority (of kings, husbands, clergy) comes from God; used to justify women's subordination; rejected by the authors (Ch 1, Ch 15)

**Domestic Sphere** — The idea that women's proper place is in the home; challenged by suffrage advocates who argued women needed broader participation (Introduction)

**Education** — Central to women's emancipation; education frees the mind from bondage of authority and enables self-assertion (Ch 1)

**Emancipation** — Liberation from oppression; the goal of the women's suffrage movement, parallel to the abolition of slavery (Introduction)

**Fugitive Slave Law** — Law requiring the return of escaped slaves; women's rights advocates often worked with abolitionists against this law (Ch 10)

**Government** — In a republic based on individual conscience, women argued they must have equal voice in government (Introduction)

**Industrial Revolution** — Period that created new employment opportunities for women, though often at unequal pay (Ch 1)

**Inquisition** — Historical period of religious persecution; used as analogy for the persecution of women who challenged authority (Ch 15)

**Legislature** — Law-making body; women sought the right to petition and testify before legislatures, and ultimately to vote for legislators (Ch 4, Ch 5, Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 12, Ch 14)

**Lucretia Mott** — Quaker minister, abolitionist, and women's rights leader; co-organizer of Seneca Falls Convention (Ch 4, Ch 11)

**Marriage** — Legal institution that historically gave husbands complete control over wives' property and persons; reform of marriage laws was a key women's rights issue (Ch 1, Ch 15)

**Medical College for Women** — First opened in 1850 in Pennsylvania; represented women's entry into professions (Ch 10)

**Natural Rights** — Rights inherent to all human beings by nature, not granted by government; central to the women's rights argument (Introduction)

**New Jersey Constitution** — Notably allowed some women to vote in the colonial period; later restricted (Ch 12)

**Pariahs** — In the caste analogy, women occupy the position of Pariahs (the lowest caste), while men are Brahmins (Ch 1)

**Political Rights** — The right to vote, hold office, and participate in government; the central demand of the suffrage movement (Introduction, Ch 1)

**Property Rights** — Legal rights to own, control, and inherit property; Married Women's Property Acts (beginning in New York, 1848) were early victories for women's rights (Ch 1, Ch 14)

**Protection** — The argument that women do not need rights because men will protect them; rejected by Stanton, Anthony, and Gage as the "excuse of tyrants" (Ch 1, Introduction)

**Pulpit** — The platform for religious teaching; women were denied access to the pulpit, reinforcing theological authority over them (Ch 1, Ch 15)

**Quakers** — Religious group known for equality of the sexes in their practices; many early women's rights advocates were Quakers (Lucretia Mott, Ch 11)

**Reformation** — Historical religious movement; used as example of progress that women's rights would follow (Ch 1)

**Religion** — Used historically to justify women's subordination; the authors argue that religious principles actually support equality (Introduction, Ch 1, Ch 15)

**Separate Spheres** — Ideology that men and women have different, non-overlapping roles in society; challenged by the suffrage movement (Introduction, Ch 1)

**Seneca Falls Convention (1848)** — First women's rights convention in the United States, held in Seneca Falls, New York; adopted the Declaration of Sentiments (Ch 4)

**Slave Codes** — Laws controlling the behavior and rights of enslaved people; compared to laws controlling women (Ch 1)

**Social Revolution** — Feared consequence of women's equality by opponents; the authors argue true equality prevents rather than causes revolution (Introduction)

**Susan B. Anthony** — Teacher, abolitionist, and women's rights leader; co-editor of History of Woman Suffrage; known for organizing and strategic skills (Preface, Ch 13)

**Temperance Movement** — Social movement against alcohol; many women were active in temperance before and alongside suffrage work (Ch 7, Ch 8, Ch 14)

**Una** — Newspaper edited by Paulina Wright Davis; early women's rights publication (Ch 8)

**Witchcraft** — Historical phenomenon where women were disproportionately accused and executed; used as example of religious persecution of women (Ch 15)

**Women's Rights Convention** — Meetings held in various states to organize for women's suffrage and rights; Seneca Falls (1848) was the first (Ch 4, Ch 6, Ch 8, Ch 10, Ch 14)
