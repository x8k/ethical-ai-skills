## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Principle of Equal Rights
**Use when**: Arguing for political equality, suffrage, or legal rights
**Key Insight**: "All men are created equal" — if this principle is accepted, then excluding half of humanity (women) from political rights is a contradiction. Equal rights are not a privilege to be granted but a fundamental principle of democracy.
**Application**: Test any restriction on women's rights against democratic principles. If men have a right, women must have the same right unless a compelling, non-arbitrary reason exists.

### Argument from Justice
**Use when**: Advocating for women's enfranchisement on moral grounds
**Key Insight**: Justice requires that those subject to laws have a voice in making them, and those whose property or labor is taxed have representation. Denying women suffrage violates these fundamental principles.
**Application**: Use when opponents claim women's exclusion is tradition or natural. Counter with: if women must obey laws and pay taxes, they must have representation.

### Argument from Expediency
**Use when**: Addressing practical concerns about women's participation
**Key Insight**: Women's inclusion in political and professional life is not only just but beneficial. Societies that include women's talents, perspectives, and labor are more productive and harmonious.
**Application**: Counter objections about disruption by showing how inclusion improves governance, economic output, and social stability.

### Refutation of Objections
**Use when**: Responding to common arguments against women's rights
**Key Insight**: The three main objections — maternity's incompatibility with public life, increased competition depressing wages, and hardening of character — are all groundless. Each can be systematically refuted with evidence and logic.
**Application**: When facing resistance, use the structured refutations: maternity argument is individual not universal, competition benefits society overall, and "hardening" reflects outdated gender stereotypes.

### Historical Precedent for Change
**Use when**: Overcoming the "it's always been this way" objection
**Key Insight**: Every expansion of rights (abolition of slavery, religious tolerance, democratic reforms) was once considered radical and impossible. The subjection of women follows the same pattern and will similarly be overcome.
**Application**: Use historical examples (end of slavery, religious freedom, democratic revolutions) to show that what seems immutable can change.

### Power and Virtue Analysis
**Use when**: Examining how power structures shape moral concepts
**Key Insight**: "Power makes itself the centre of moral obligation." The powerful define virtues for the powerless to serve their interests. Male virtues emphasize self-assertion; female virtues emphasize submission — both serve male dominance.
**Application**: Analyze any gendered moral concept by asking: who benefits from this definition of virtue? How does it maintain existing power structures?

### Domestic Power Dynamics
**Use when**: Understanding the evolution of marriage and domestic relations
**Key Insight**: Domestic relations have evolved from women as slaves (physical labor) to women as companions (emotional service). Modern relations require genuine partnership, but existing power imbalances prevent this.
**Application**: Use to analyze marital conflict, domestic inequality, and the need for legal reforms in marriage.

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers Harriet Taylor Mill's "The Enfranchisement of Women" (1851), originally published in the Westminster and Foreign Quarterly Review. It provides frameworks for arguing women's political rights, analyzing objections to gender equality, and understanding the historical development of women's rights movements.

For modern applications, note that this essay predates major feminist developments. Combine with contemporary gender theory for complete analysis. For implementation in specific political or legal contexts, add domain-specific knowledge.
