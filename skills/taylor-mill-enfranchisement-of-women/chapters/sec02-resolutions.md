# Section II: The Resolutions

## Core Idea
The convention adopted resolutions that took a principled, uncompromising stand on women's rights. These were not limited demands but a comprehensive assertion of equality "in all rights, political, civil and social" with men.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Principled Advocacy**: Taking a stand on principles without time-serving or compromise, declaring rights "in their widest extent."
- **Comprehensive Equality Demand**: Not partial reforms but full equality across all domains.
- **Universal Application**: Rights claims apply to all women, not just educated or propertied women.

## Key Concepts
- **Resolution 1**: Every adult resident subject to laws is entitled to a voice in their enactment.
- **Resolution 2**: Every person whose property or labor is taxed is entitled to representation.
- **Resolution 3**: Women are entitled to suffrage and office eligibility.
- **Resolution 4**: Civil and political rights acknowledge no sex — "male" should be stricken from state constitutions.
- **Resolution 5**: Equal access to education and professions is essential for women to develop their faculties.

## Mental Models
- **Rights as universal**: If a right is valid for one group, it must be valid for all unless a justified distinction exists.
- **Taxation requires representation**: A fundamental democratic principle that applies to women as well as men.
- **Capability through opportunity**: Abilities develop through exercise; restriction prevents development.

## Anti-patterns
- **Incrementalism**: Accepting partial rights as a final goal rather than a step toward full equality.
- **Qualified support**: Supporting rights for some women (educated, propertied) but not all.
- **Apologetic advocacy**: Softening demands to make them more palatable to opponents.

## Key Takeaways
1. The resolutions asserted broad, principled demands for full equality.
2. Suffrage was claimed as a right, not requested as a favor.
3. The principle of equal rights applies regardless of sex, just as it should regardless of color.
4. Existing democratic principles already support women's equality claims.
5. The movement refused to limit its demands based on what seemed politically feasible.
