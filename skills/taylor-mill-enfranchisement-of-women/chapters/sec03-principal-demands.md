# Section III: Principal Demands

## Core Idea
The movement articulated three principal demands that represented the practical application of equality principles: education, partnership, and political participation.

## Frameworks Introduced

- **Structural Equality**: Equal rights require structural changes in education, economics, and politics.
- **Multi-Dimensional Reform**: Equality cannot be achieved through political rights alone; economic and educational reforms are also necessary.

## Key Concepts
- **Education**: Access to all levels of education, from primary schools to universities, including professional training in medicine, law, and theology.
- **Partnership**: Equal sharing in the labors, gains, risks, and remunerations of productive industry with men.
- **Co-equal Share**: Equal participation in forming and administering laws at all levels (municipal, state, national) through legislative assemblies, courts, and executive offices.

## Mental Models
- **Education as foundation**: Without equal education, women cannot compete equally or develop their full potential.
- **Economic independence**: Partnership in industry provides the material basis for genuine equality.
- **Political power**: Without representation in government, women cannot shape the laws that govern their lives.

## Anti-patterns
- **Separate spheres**: Arguing that women belong only in the domestic realm.
- **Token inclusion**: Accepting a few exceptional women in male domains without systemic change.
- **Sequential reform**: Delaying one form of equality until another is achieved.

## Key Takeaways
1. Three demands form the core of women's equality: education, economic partnership, political participation.
2. These demands are interrelated and mutually reinforcing.
3. Education enables women to develop capabilities for other spheres.
4. Economic partnership provides independence and dignity.
5. Political participation ensures women's interests are represented in governance.
