## Core Frameworks & Mental Models

### The Room of One's Own Framework
**Use when**: Analyzing the material conditions necessary for creative work
- **Core principle**: "A woman must have money and a room of her own if she is to write fiction"
- **Components**: 
  - **Money**: Financial independence (£500 a year) provides freedom from economic necessity
  - **Room**: Physical space provides freedom from interruption and obligation
- **Purpose**: These create the mental and temporal space for concentrated thought and creation
- **Application**: This principle extends beyond writing to all forms of intellectual work

### Shakespeare's Sister (Judith Shakespeare)
**Use when**: Understanding the historical barriers to women's creative expression
- **Core principle**: Imagine Shakespeare had an equally gifted sister; what would her life have been?
- **The Thought Experiment**: 
  - Same genius, same age, same circumstances
  - Denied education, denied opportunity, denied independence
  - Forced into marriage, her genius suppressed and wasted
- **Purpose**: Reveals the systemic barriers that prevented women from achieving literary greatness
- **Application**: Highlights how talent without opportunity is as useless as opportunity without talent

### The Androgynous Mind
**Use when**: Analyzing the ideal creative temperament
- **Core principle**: "It is fatal for anyone who writes to think of their sex. It is fatal to be a man or woman pure and simple; one must be woman-manly or man-womanly"
- **The Androgynous Ideal**: The creative mind contains both masculine and feminine elements in harmony
- **Barrier**: When one sex dominates or is conscious of being a particular sex, creativity is inhibited
- **Historical evidence**: The great writers (Shakespeare, Jane Austen, etc.) had androgynous minds
- **Application**: Great writing requires the ability to move beyond gendered perspectives

### The Angel in the House
**Use when**: Understanding the internalized oppression of women writers
- **Core principle**: The idealized Victorian woman (the Angel in the House) was a barrier to women's creative expression
- **Mechanism**: Women writers had to kill this internalized ideal of pure, self-sacrificing femininity
- **Process**: This killing of the Angel was a necessary act of liberation for women writers
- **Application**: Internalized oppression must be confronted before external barriers can be overcome

### The Economic Argument
**Use when**: Analyzing how economic conditions affect intellectual output
- **Core principle**: Material conditions fundamentally shape what can be written
- **Historical observation**: Most writing by women before the 20th century was about women's grievances because those were their lived experiences
- **Mechanism**: Economic dependence forces women to write about what concerns their economic position
- **Solution**: Economic independence allows women to write about the full range of human experience

### The Intellectual Freedom Framework
**Use when**: Advocating for women's access to education and resources
- **Core principle**: "Lock up your libraries if you like; but there is no gate, no lock, no bolt that you can set upon the freedom of my mind"
- **Mechanism**: While physical barriers can be erected, the mind's freedom cannot be absolutely constrained
- **Application**: The freedom to read, think, and write is fundamental to human dignity

### The Historical Analysis of Women's Writing
**Use when**: Understanding the development of women's literature
- **Core principle**: Women's writing has developed through stages as conditions changed
- **Stages**: 
  - **Silence**: No writing by women (pre-16th century)
  - **Apology**: Writing that apologizes for women's existence (16th-18th centuries)
  - **Protest**: Writing about women's grievances (19th century)
  - **Freedom**: Writing on any subject (20th century onwards)
- **Application**: Each stage reflects the degree of freedom and opportunity available to women

### The Male Sex as Subject Framework
**Use when**: Analyzing gender dynamics in literature
- **Core principle**: Men write endlessly about women, but women rarely write about men
- **Observation**: The British Museum catalogue shows vast numbers of books about women written by men
- **Implication**: Women have been the object of study, not the subject writing about others
- **Application**: This reveals the power imbalance in intellectual life

### The Mirror Metaphor
**Use when**: Understanding the psychological function of women in patriarchal society
- **Core principle**: "Women have served all these centuries as looking-glasses possessing the magic and delicious power of reflecting the figure of man at twice its natural size"
- **Mechanism**: By reflecting men at twice their natural size, women have enabled men's sense of superiority and heroism
- **Consequence**: This function has been essential to "all violent and heroic action" in history
- **Application**: When women cease to function as mirrors, men's sense of superiority is threatened

## Scope & Limits

This skill covers Virginia Woolf's extended essay "A Room of One's Own" (1929), based on lectures delivered at Newnham College and Girton College, Cambridge.

The essay examines the historical, economic, and social barriers to women's writing and intellectual freedom. It combines personal reflection, historical analysis, and literary criticism.

For practical application, combine this skill with contemporary feminist theory, economic analysis, and literary studies.
