# Glossary — A Room of One's Own

**Angel in the House** — The Victorian ideal of the pure, self-sacrificing, domesticated woman; coined by Coventry Patmore. Woolf argues women writers must kill this internalized ideal to achieve creative freedom (Ch 5)

**Androgynous Mind** — Woolf's concept of the ideal creative mind that contains both masculine and feminine elements in harmony. The mind that is "woman-manly or man-womanly" can achieve greatness because it transcends gendered perspectives (Ch 3, Ch 6)

**British Museum** — Major research library in London where Woolf conducts research on women and fiction. The visit reveals the vast number of books about women written by men, and the scarcity of books about men written by women (Ch 2)

**£500 a Year** — Woolf's calculation of the minimum income needed for a woman to have financial independence and thus the freedom to write. This figure comes from her observation that £500 is what allows a person to live comfortably without economic anxiety (Ch 1)

**Fernham** — Fictional women's college in Woolf's narrative, based on Newnham and Girton Colleges at Cambridge. Represents the economic and social constraints faced by women's education (Ch 1)

**Fiction** — For Woolf, fiction has the potential to contain more truth than fact because it can express inner realities and subjective experiences that factual reporting cannot capture (Ch 1)

**Judith Shakespeare** — Woolf's fictional sister of William Shakespeare, used as a thought experiment to illustrate the barriers faced by talented women in Shakespeare's time. Judith has the same genius but is denied education, opportunity, and independence (Ch 3)

**Looking-Glass** — Woolf's metaphor for women's historical role in patriarchal society: "looking-glasses possessing the magic and delicious power of reflecting the figure of man at twice its natural size." Women's function as mirrors has been essential to men's sense of superiority and heroic achievement (Ch 3)

**Mirror Stage** — Woolf's analysis of how women function psychologically in patriarchal society by reflecting men at twice their natural size, thereby enabling men's sense of grandeur and achievement (Ch 3)

**Newnham College** — One of the two women's colleges at Cambridge University where Woolf delivered the lectures that became "A Room of One's Own." Founded in 1871 (Ch Introduction)

**Girton College** — The other women's college at Cambridge where Woolf delivered lectures. Founded in 1869 (Ch Introduction)

**Oxbridge** — Woolf's fictional combination of Oxford and Cambridge, used to critique the male-dominated academic institutions that excluded women (Ch 1)

**Patriarchy** — Social system in which men hold primary power and predominate in roles of political leadership, moral authority, and economic privilege. Woolf analyzes how patriarchy affects women's intellectual freedom (Ch 2, Ch 3, Ch 5)

**Room of One's Own** — Woolf's central metaphor for the material conditions necessary for creative work: both a physical space free from interruption and the financial independence (£500 a year) that makes such space possible (Ch 1)

**Shakespeare's Sister** — See Judith Shakespeare. This thought experiment is Woolf's most powerful illustration of the historical barriers to women's creative expression (Ch 3)

**Truth** — For Woolf, truth is complex and multi-faceted. Fiction can express truths that factual reporting cannot. The search for truth requires intellectual freedom and the ability to move beyond simplistic binaries (Throughout)

**Women and Fiction** — The subject of Woolf's lectures and essay. This phrase can mean: women and what they are like; women and the fiction they write; women and the fiction written about them; or all three inextricably mixed together (Ch 1)

**Women's College** — Educational institutions for women, such as Newnham and Girton at Cambridge. Woolf uses these to illustrate both the progress of women's education and its continuing economic limitations (Ch 1)

**Women's Writing** — Literature written by women. Woolf traces its historical development from silence to apology to protest to freedom (Ch 3, Ch 4)
